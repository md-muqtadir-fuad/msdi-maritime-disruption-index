Abstract
Container shipping disruptions impose substantial costs on global supply chains, yet tools to
measure them in real time remain limited. Established news-based indicators rely on simple
keyword counting, which cannot capture the specific language of maritime disruption or
distinguish a genuinely critical headline from one that merely mentions trade. This study
addresses that gap by building and validating the first container shipping disruption index
derived from news headlines using a large language model. A corpus of 5,586 headlines
spanning January 2015 to December 2025 was classified by an open-source, instruction-tuned
language model against an established maritime disruption framework of two dimensions and
seven themes, producing a graded relevance score for each headline rather than a yes-or-no
match. These scores were aggregated into a monthly index after filtering out irrelevant
coverage, then validated against the SCFI, the standard benchmark for container shipping costs.
The index tracks freight rates closely, and the relationship strengthens markedly during acute
disruptions while fading in calm periods. A time-series analysis reveals a clear, one-directional
pattern: freight rate movements lead disruption coverage by one to two months, whereas
coverage carries no predictive power for future rates. The media therefore reflects shipping
shocks rather than anticipating them. The study delivers a transparent, reproducible tool for
measuring disruption intensity and new evidence on how disruption signals travel between
markets and news.
Keywords: Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI), Disruption Index (DI).

1 Introduction
Container shipping is the key channel of international merchandise trade. The United Nations
Conference on Trade and Development emphasizes that maritime transport carries the
overwhelming majority of internationally traded goods by volume, and within this system
containerized liner shipping is responsible for the great bulk of manufactured cargoes that move
between continents (Unctad, 2021). This seamless operation of this network is in the normal
operation of things accepted as a virtually invisible precondition of the contemporary
economical life: the factories get the parts at the right time, the shelves are always replenished,
and the consumers do not face the lack of goods or the sudden increase or decrease of their
price that happen to cross the continents. Only this disturbance of the system leads to its
centrality being seen. The years 2015-2025 have provided a comparatively abundant list of
such upheavals - the COVID-19 pandemic and the Suez Canal blockage of March 2021, the
Shanghai port lockdown of 2022 and the Red Sea transit lockdown since the end of 2023 -
uncovering how ill-equipped the current analytical equipment is to quantify the severity, nature
and duration of shipping disruption in continuous time.


The COVID-19 pandemic represents the most significant shipping likely to have happened in
recent history. Factory shutdowns in early 2020 cascaded into uneven port demand and severe
equipment imbalances, container vessels became trapped on the wrong side of trade lanes, and
dwell times at major hubs rose substantially (Unctad, 2021). Khan and colleagues (Khan et al.,
2022) document the pronounced volatility this generated in containerized freight indices using
quantile-on-quantile methods . Nevertheless, the disruption was not some singular shock but
rather a series of shocks that interacted over a span of over two years and the post pandemic
period has been no less turbulent. The blockade of the Suez Canal, the closure of the Shanghai
port and the capacity reallocation and the freight rate pressure caused by the Houthi and the
disruption of Red Sea transit and its subsequent repositioning around the Cape of Good Hope
each caused new waves of reallocation of capacities and the pressure of freight. These outbursts
underscore one key point shipping disruption is not an isolated historical incident but a
recurrent aspect of modern international trade, and the resulting impacts on freight markets,
downstream producing also take their quantifiable toll and impacts on consumer welfare.


With such disruptions being undoubtedly of great economic significance, the analytical
infrastructure to quantify them in real time is, surprisingly, rather minimal. The regularly
quoted freight indices, such as the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI), Baltic Dry

                                                2
Index, Harpex Container Index, reflect the market price of shipping capacity but only as a
reaction to disruption as opposed to disruption. The existence of spike in freight rates is
confirmation that something is going on in the market; it does not reveal to the analyst what in
the underlying supply chain is the most active of the themes of disruption or whether the system
is being strained how long. News coverage, in contrast, carries with it abundant amounts of
this contextual information, but in an unstructured format which is hard to put into a
quantitative time series. The missing element has been a continuous, theme-decomposed and
machine-readable index of the intensity of disruption which draws upon the open universe of
modern news coverage, and which is calibrated against time-tested freight norms. The
construction of such an index is the central concern of this thesis. The intellectual precedent is
the Economic Policy Uncertainty Index of Baker and colleagues (Baker et al., 2016a), which
demonstrates that newspaper coverage can be converted into a continuous quantitative measure
for an intangible economic phenomenon — in their case, policy uncertainty. The technological
facilitator is the fast maturation of open-source large language models, which have now made
it feasible to classify large amounts of free text against a structured taxonomy without incurring
proprietary application-programming-interface costs and without the manual coding cost that
would otherwise be prohibitive on scale. Combining these two developments with a recent
qualitative taxonomy of container shipping disruption developed by Munim (Munim, 2025),
this thesis produces a monthly disruption index for the period 2015 to 2025 and tests its
statistical relationship with the SCFI using standard time-series econometric methods.


The problem that the given research will address can be summarized in a few words. The
available literature includes on the one hand qualitative taxonomies of container shipping
disruption as a result of thematic analysis of news reports on certain crises; on the other hand,
freight indices to measure the market value of the shipping capacity; and, independently, news-
based economic indices to other related areas such as macroeconomic policy uncertainty. The
only thing it lacks to the best of our knowledge is a continuous, theme-decomposed, news-
based disruption index that is specific to container shipping and has been built in a manner that
can be fully replicated by other researchers using commodity hardware and without access to
proprietary data.


The timeliness of this thesis is apt on two fronts. The qualitative groundwork is now in place:
Munim (Munim, 2025) provides a thematic taxonomy of container shipping disruption derived
from systematic analysis of news articles during the COVID-19 crisis, identifying the
                                                3
dimensions, themes, and codes that distinguish disruption-relevant content from other
coverage. The technical foundations are also ready: open-source instruction-tuned language
models are now able to perform research-grade classification on hardware cost accessible even
to undergraduate researchers, eliminating the major bottleneck of large-scale taxonomy-
directed text classification. It is the gap of how to reconcile the two developments, to produce
a reproducible monthly index, to be checked against a current freight index, that this thesis fills.
The literature behind each of these assertions is described in some detail and a formal mapping
of each of the preceding contributions into the respective element of the current methodology
is given in the literature review


The primary objective of this work is to construct a reproducible, continuous monthly index of
container shipping disruption intensity for the period 2015 to 2025, derived from large
language model-based classification of news headlines against the Munim (Munim, 2025)
taxonomy, and to validate this index econometrically against the SCFI.
This thesis can benefit us in three ways. Methodologically, it shows that, sector-specific
disruption index can be entirely created using the free-open-source components on the freely-
available cloud infrastructure, to overcome a question of reproducibility that lingers over a
body of recent literature on the use of language-model-based financial and supply chain text
analysis. The task of injecting the language model with instructions based on an existing
qualitative taxonomy, absent of finetuning, demonstrates a tendency of "domain knowledge
through prompting" which can be likely generalized to other sectoral implementations.


The thesis constructs an empirically eligible operationalization of an earlier qualitative
taxonomy into a monthly time series with continuous values and an econometric description of
the dynamic interaction between this index and the best box freight reference point. Extending
news-based index approach to the maritime space, the thesis contributes to the small, yet
recently growing, research base that quantifies sector-specific disruption effects of media
content. In practice, the resulting index can be effectively used as an analyst, policymaker, and
supply chain manager of the descriptive continuous measure of shipping-sector stress. The
theme-level decomposition also allows the practitioners to understand which channels of
disruption are being most active at any point such as port congestion, container availability or
freight cost, which are not possible to do when using aggregate freight indices. The pipeline,
dataset, and prompt design can be replicated in full in zero monetary cost and thus reduce the
entry cost of future researcher building up on this work.

2 Literature Review
Monthly news-based disruption index of container shipping is at the crossroads of four strands
of research that until recently have been developed in relative isolation: the news-based
economic indices tradition, the text-based sentiment and media-finance tradition, the newly
flourishing large language model (LLM) literature in financial and supply chain text analysis,
and the empirical literature on the economics of container shipping. Methodological validation
of such an index also capitalizes on a fifth body of literature; that is the time-series econometric
literature. In this chapter, each of these streams is briefly reviewed, which also serves the
purpose of illustrating the methodological background upon which the current thesis is based
as well as the gap that inspired it.




                                                 5
The review is structured in a way that it brings about the necessity of the next section. Section
2.1 examines the news-based economic indices literature, anchored in the Economic Policy
Uncertainty (EPU) Index of Baker, Bloom and Davis (Baker et al., 2016a), which establishes
the foundational premise that newspaper coverage can be converted into a continuous
quantitative measure. Section 2.2 reviews the text-based sentiment and media-finance
literature, in which Tetlock (Tetlock, 2007), Tetlock and colleagues (Tetlock et al., 2008),
Garcia (Garcia, 2013), Loughran and McDonald (Loughran & McDonald, 2011), and Arratia
and colleagues (Arratia et al., 2021) collectively establish what information such indices carry,
when this information is most useful, and how it should be aggregated and smoothed. Section
2.4 turns to the recent and rapidly maturing literature on LLMs as tools for text classification
in financial and supply chain settings, in which Kim, Muhn and Nikolaev (Kim et al., 2023),
Lopez-Lira and Tang (Lopez-Lira & Tang, 2023), Fang and colleagues (Fang et al., 2026), and
Yang and colleagues at the Qwen Team (Yang & Qwen, 2024) document a clear informational
and operational advantage over keyword and dictionary methods. Section 2.5 reviews container
shipping economics, with emphasis on COVID-19 era disruptions and the maritime disruption
taxonomy of Munim (Munim, 2025) grounded in the thematic analysis methodology of Braun
and Clarke (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Section 2.6 is a survey of time-series econometric
techniques employed to test the resulting index. Section 2.7 then officially marks the gap in the
research and traces every previous contribution to the appropriate aspect of the methodology
constructed in section 3 and Section 2.8 brings the chapter to an end.


This literature review is limited to peer-reviewed journal articles, working papers published in
reputable research institutions, official publication of policies by intergovernmental
organizations and authoritative econometric monographs. In cases where assertions are made
based on a best-of-knowledge evaluation as opposed to a direct verification quote, it is made
plain in the text as opposed to hiding a citation that tries to place excess confidence in its ability
to be verified.


2.1 News-Based Economic Indices
The methodological history behind this thesis is that of the news-based literature of economic
indices, whereby text-based reports of an economic phenomenon are transformed into a
continuous, quantitative time series that is amenable to econometric analysis. The canonical
contribution is the Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) Index of Baker, Bloom and Davis


                                                  6
(Baker et al., 2016a), which has become a reference point for subsequent text-based index
construction across multiple economic domains.


2.1.1 The Economic Policy Uncertainty Index
Baker and colleagues (Baker et al., 2016a) construct a monthly index of policy-related
economic uncertainty using newspaper coverage from ten major United States newspapers.
Article counts are calculated on the coincidence of sets of keywords consisting of the terms:
economic, policy and uncertainty. The resultant counts are then divided by the total number of
articles in order to adjust differences between newspaper sizes and amount of historical
coverage and averaged across the newspapers to yield a monthly time series. Next, the authors
test EPU index versus other measures of economic uncertainty such as the Chicago Board
Options Exchange Volatility Index and show that EPU innovations augment a nontrivial
proportion of forecast error variance in investment and output in a panel of twelve countries.


This work directly builds the index based on three methodological aspects of the EPU
framework. First, the structure creates a premise that the versus of newspapers can be
transformed into a continuous measure of a quantitative nature, which cannot be analyzed by
econometrics, as long as the set of keywords targeted the phenomenon of interest. Second, it
makes text-based indices institutionalized to be validated against the known market standards
instead of viewing it as independent measurement. Third, it gives unapologetically open
consideration to the possibility that news-based measures are more of a reflection than a cause
of economic outcomes - an interpretive caveat that is likewise apparent in the literature
surveyed in Section 2.3 and has been subsequently reiterated literally in the empirical results
of section 4.


The principal methodological limitation of the EPU approach, acknowledged by Baker and
colleagues (Baker et al., 2016a) themselves, is its reliance on simple keyword matching. The
classification based on keywords is unable to distinguish between those articles in which the
terms of interest occur in the middle and those in which they occur by chance, as well as those
in which the underlying phenomenon is discussed by other words. In this study, the current
thesis will overcome this limitation, substituting keyword matching with large language model
classification against a structured taxonomy, which has been reviewed in Section 2.4.




                                               7
2.1.2 Implications for a Sector-Specific Maritime Index

The EPU approach has been scaled down to many areas and country specific conditions in the
latter literature. None such continuous, news-based disruption index of container shipping is
available in the literature we have reviewed to develop this thesis to the best of our knowledge.
The UNCTAD Policy Brief on COVID-19 era container shipping (Unctad, 2021) documents
the unprecedented surge in container freight rates and outlines policy implications for shipping
disruption monitoring, but does not itself constitute a continuous measurement instrument. This
fact, paired with the proven viability of the news-driven approach to the index exhibited by the
EPU literature, inspires the sector-specific maritime disruption index developed in section 3.

2.2 Text-Based Sentiment and the Media-Finance Literature

Although the EPU framework confirms that news-based indices are viable, the general media-
finite literature offers theoretical underpinnings to what information such indices can entail,
when such information is the most valuable and whether the information is aggregated. In this
section, four contributions of this literature are reviewed that directly influence the design of
the disruption index: continuous sentiment extraction, regime-dependent informativeness,
domain adaptation and temporal smoothing.


2.2.1 Continuous Sentiment Extraction
The foundational study in the media-finance literature is Tetlock (Tetlock, 2007), which
extracts a continuous pessimism factor from the daily Wall Street Journal “Abreast of the
Market” column using word-frequency analysis based on the Harvard IV-4 General Inquirer
dictionary. The author records that high media pessimism foretells market prices in downward
movements followed by a way back to the fundamentals and that supernormal pessimism or
abnormally excessive or low pessimism foretells high performing market trading volume.
Two methodological insights from Tetlock (Tetlock, 2007) directly motivate the design of the
disruption index. The first is the application of a continuous measure of sentiment as opposed
to a binary positive-negative measure, which maintains the gradient in the meaning which
headlines and articles reflect. The second, and perhaps more significant, is the concomitant
effect of intensity and range of coverage of information on the transmission of economic
information: a small number of strongly negative articles, but large number of moderately
negative articles, can have different content, and both measures need to be kept in any
aggregate index. This two-level construction is explicitly a stimulus to the intensity-by-volume
formulation of the disruption index formulated in Chapter 3.

                                               8
Tetlock, Saar-Tsechansky and Macskassy (Tetlock et al., 2008) extend this framework by
processing approximately three hundred and fifty thousand Dow Jones News Service stories to
extract firm-level sentiment, demonstrating that the linguistic content of news predicts both
firm earnings and stock returns. The size of their analysis makes it clear that large-scale
automated text processing of news content is not only feasible but informative, as a precedent
that helps the batch-processing pipeline proposed in this thesis to classify the 5,586 headlines
between the years 2015 and 2025.


2.2.2 Regime-Dependent Informativeness
A somewhat pertinent line of the media-finance literature is the informativeness of news-based
measures that vary with the regime. Garcia (Garcia, 2013) documents that the predictive power
of news-based sentiment for stock market returns is concentrated in recession periods, with
substantially weaker signal during expansionary phases. The implication of this observation on
the current thesis is as follows: since the disruption freight relationship is itself regime
dependent, the information content of the news based index would be greatest at times of acute
disruption and less at times of calm.


This expectation of the maritime domain is confirmed by the rolling-weighted correlation
analysis in Chapter 4. Correlation between disruption and freight is significantly increased
during the COVID-19 crisis and zero in the intervening peaceful periods. To the best of our
knowledge, this pattern has not previously been documented in the maritime domain, and it
constitutes one of the more substantive empirical extensions of this thesis from the equity-
market findings of Garcia (Garcia, 2013) into the container shipping setting.


2.3.3 Domain Adaptation and the Limits of Generic Dictionaries
Loughran and McDonald (Loughran & McDonald, 2011) demonstrate that general-purpose
sentiment dictionaries perform poorly when applied to financial texts. About three-quarters of
the words that the Harvard IV-4 dictionary calls negative do not mean negative in a monetary
sense - a false rate that is enough to significantly bias measures of sentiment based on the
generic word list. The authors develop a domain specific financial sentiment dictionary that
does very well in clustering financial disclosures compared to the Harvard dictionary.


The methodological implication to the current thesis is that domain is required to be adapted
when applying any of the approaches to text-classification to a specialized domain like
                                               9
container shipping. Terminology of maritime disruption - blank sailings, demurrage, TEU
imbalance, transshipment, port congestion - is specialized enough that a classification solution
based on generic English-language word lists will tend to be poor. The present thesis achieves
domain adaptation not through a custom dictionary but through prompt-engineered taxonomy
injection in a large language model, an approach that combines the domain-specificity principle
of Loughran and McDonald (Loughran & McDonald, 2011) with the contextual reasoning
capacity reviewed in Section 2.4.


2.3.4 Temporal Smoothing of Sentiment Indices
The use of exponential moving average smoothing in the index construction follows the
practice documented by Arratia, Avalos and Renedo-Mirambell (Arratia et al., 2021) in their
treatment of sentiment analysis of financial news. The authors remark that the raw monthly
sentiment indices are normally characterized with significant editorial noise that distorts the
underlying signal, and that short-window smoothing will enhance the signal-to-noise ratio
without significantly weakening the information of a crisis period. The three-month
exponential moving average used in the present thesis represents a moderate smoothing
window that retains crisis-period responsiveness while suppressing month-to-month noise of
the kind discussed by Arratia and colleagues (Arratia et al., 2021).


2.3 Large Language Models for Financial and Supply Chain Text Analysis
Venturing into the financial and supply chain text analysis using large language models (LLMs)
is a relatively new but quickly growing area of research. This subsection discusses three sets
of this literature consecutively: the empirical demonstration of the informational advantage of
the use of LLMs over a keyword and dictionary paradigm, the most immediate methodological
antecedent of the structured-prompt method found in this thesis, and the reasons that lead to
the selection of an open-source model.


2.3.1 LLMs as Financial Text Classifiers
Kim, Muhn and Nikolaev (Kim et al., 2023) demonstrate that LLM-derived measures of
corporate risk extracted from earnings conference call transcripts outperform traditional bag-
of-words approaches in predicting market volatility, firm-level investment decisions and
capital structure choices. . The authors emphasize that Conditional language, future tense
forms, and subjunctive constructions are contextual features of language that token-frequency


                                              10
methods fail to capture, yet are captured by LLMs. The results of this study demonstrate that,
in financial contexts, the use of LLM-based classification offers a real informational benefit to
keyword and dictionary methods, as well as to the more broadly speaking conclusions of the
media-finance literature surveyed in Section 2.3.


Lopez-Lira and Tang (Lopez-Lira & Tang, 2023) provide complementary evidence using a
proprietary LLM to score the sentiment of news headlines and predict subsequent stock returns.
The authors report that sentiment scores that are generated by the LLM generate positive
abnormal returns when utilized as trading signals, especially in smaller firms. Even though this
result confirms that information contained in the LLM-derived signals of news headlines, at
least relative to those of longer corporate disclosures, are specific to that information, the result
also demonstrates a recurring practical problem of this literature: studies that utilize proprietary
application-programming-interface models are upheld by infrastructure whose internal
weights, version history and access conditions are not available to the researcher in all respects.
This is the reason behind a conscious decision to adopt an open-source model in the current
thesis as 2.4.3 will discuss below.


2.3.2 Structured Prompting for Supply Chain Risk
The most directly relevant LLM application for the present thesis is Fang, Su, Wang and Wang
(Fang et al., 2026), published in the International Journal of Production Economics, which
develops an LLM framework for identifying digital supply chain risks from unstructured
corporate disclosures. The authors develop structured prompt templates that direct GPT-based
models to derive standardized and interpretable risk indicators and discover that the measures
derived by the LLM can explain ten to fifteen percent more variance in firm-level market
volatility, digital investment and inventory choices compared with traditional bag-of-words
models. Importantly to the current thesis, the authors underline that such instability and
interpretability issues that are plagued by zero-shot analyses of semantically complex and
narrative business texts can be but alleviated by structured prompting.


The methodological pattern established by Fang and colleagues (Fang et al., 2026) — namely,
the injection of explicit domain knowledge and structured output requirements into the LLM
prompt rather than into model fine-tuning — directly motivates the structured prompt design
adopted in this thesis. As detailed in Chapter 3, the prompt embeds the complete Munim
(Munim, 2025) taxonomy, an explicit zero-to-ten scoring rubric, a constrained theme
                                                 11
vocabulary and a fixed output schema, following exactly the principle that domain expertise
can be supplied through prompting rather than through retraining.


2.3.3 Open-Source Models and Reproducibility
Yang and colleagues at the Qwen Team (Yang & Qwen, 2024) introduce the Qwen2 model
family in a technical report from Alibaba Cloud, from which the Qwen2.5 release series
subsequently descends. The instruction-tuned version of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model applied
in this thesis is based on a variety of multilingual and code corpora that it has been trained on,
and the reported performance is close to that of much larger proprietary models on traditional
reasoning and classification tasks.


There are three reasons based on this literature that educates the decision to use Qwen2.5-7B-
Instruct instead of a closed-source option. First, open-source models can be version-locked: the
version of model weights employed in this thesis are publicly available in archives and cannot
be silently changed or changed by a provider decision that would lead to invalidation of the
experimental results. Second, the seven-billion-parameter scale is an empirical trade-off
between classification quality and computation at a scale reachable on free-tier GPU compute,
making the entire classification pipeline to be executable in the completely free cloud
infrastructure. Third, the instruction-tuned variant is specifically optimized for following
structured prompts of the kind used in this thesis and demonstrated effective by Fang and
colleagues (Fang et al., 2026).


2.4 Container Shipping Economics and the COVID 19 Disruption
The practical area of this thesis is container shipping and the quantification of disruption in this
industry over the next 10 years starting in 2015. This section provides the review of the
literature defining the empirical domain in three segments: the qualitative disruption taxonomy
that provides the classification framework, the freight-rate dynamics literature that reason the
selection of validation benchmark, and the larger literature on the supply chain crisis that
contextualizes the current one.


2.4.1 A Maritime Disruption Taxonomy
The classification taxonomy used in this thesis is drawn from Munim (Munim, 2025),
published in the Journal of Shipping and Trade. The author conducts a thematic analysis of one
hundred news articles covering the container shipping crisis during COVID-19, applying the
                                                12
established thematic analysis methodology of Braun and Clarke (Braun & Clarke, 2006), to
develop a cross-domain conceptual framework. The taxonomy differentiates between two
aggregate levels of disruption - shipping disruptions (port, global container movement, freight-
cost) and economic disruptions (supply, production, demand, COVID-19 related) and breaks
them down into seven second-order themes and seventeen first-order codes.


This taxonomy is technically useful to the current thesis because of two reasons. It is first of
all based on a thematic methodology of extracting contemporary news, that is to say that the
taxonomy is empirically stimulated precisely by the form of text that this thesis of actions
creates. Second, it is hierarchically structured, which can be easily analyzed with multi-
resolution: the LLM classifier may be requested to classify content among themes, and the
resulting index can be analyzed either in bulk or at the theme level, to produce a more narrow-
level interpretation.


Earlier project drafts attributed this taxonomy to Durugbo and Al-Balushi (Durugbo & Al-
Balushi, 2023). Independent verification confirms that Durugbo and Al-Balushi (Durugbo &
Al-Balushi, 2023) is a separate systematic review article in Management Review Quarterly,
and the specific COVID-19 container shipping taxonomy operationalized in this thesis is from
Munim (Munim, 2025). The basis of the corrected attribution in this chapter is indicated in its
reference list at the end of this chapter.


2.4.2 COVID-19 and Container Freight Rate Dynamics
Khan, Su, Khurshid and Umar (Khan et al., 2022), published in European Transport Research
Review, apply a wavelet quantile-on-quantile approach to characterize the dynamic interaction
between COVID-19 and shipping freight rates. The authors discover that the pandemic
influenced greatly the freight costs in shipping with systematic effects across quantiles of
effects of the pandemic strength and the freight rate distributions. Their analysis indicates that
linear mean-effects estimators do not sufficiently explain pandemic-era volatility in the freight
rates and that the impact of disruption shocks on freight rates depends on the intensity of a
shock and the market regime existing.


The Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI) which is the benchmark of validation in the
current thesis is a value that is highly utilized in determining the spot freight rates of container
routes that are based in Far East. The choice of the SCFI as the benchmark for the news-based
                                                13
disruption index constructed in this thesis follows the broader empirical practice of using it as
a primary indicator of container-specific freight conditions, as exemplified by the maritime
disruption literature including Khan and colleagues (Khan et al., 2022).


The UNCTAD Policy Brief on container shipping during COVID-19 (Unctad, 2021)
documents the sequence of disruptive events affecting the global container shipping network:
factory shutdowns in early 2020, port congestion and container imbalance through 2020 and
2021, and the eventual freight rate peak in early 2022. This time history may be directly
observed in the disruption index time series shown in Chapter 4 that gives a qualitative type of
construct validation of the index and the quantitative correlation of the index against the SCFI.


2.4.3 Broader Supply Chain Crisis Literature
The thematic-analysis approach used by Munim (Munim, 2025) is part of a broader resurgence
of qualitative supply chain crisis research that emerged in response to the COVID-19
disruption. Durugbo and Al-Balushi (Durugbo & Al-Balushi, 2023), in Management Review
Quarterly, conduct a systematic review of the supply chain management literature concerning
times of crisis, identifying structural factors that distinguish disruption-resilient supply chains
from disruption-vulnerable ones. Although their systematic review does not itself provide a
measurement instrument, it documents the breadth of the qualitative supply chain crisis
literature and confirms that the maritime taxonomy of Munim (Munim, 2025) is consistent with
the broader scholarly understanding of pandemic-era supply chain phenomena.


2.5 Econometric Methods for Time-Series Validation
Section 4 is based on an econometric validation of the disruption index, relying on a standard
set of time-series instruments. In this section, the methodological background is discussed in
three sections: vector autoregression and Granger causality, stationarity and cointegration
testing and lag selection.


2.5.1 Vector Autoregression and Granger Causality
The econometric framework used in Chapter 4 follows the vector autoregression (VAR)
methodology introduced by Sims (Sims, 1980). The VAR model considers both variables as
potentially endogenous, and does not impose priori constraints as to which variables cause the
others. This is especially suitable to the current thesis, in which the focal point of empirical


                                                14
inquiry is accurately whether the index of disruption based on the news predicts freight rates
or otherwise.


The Granger causality concept, formalized by Granger (Granger, 1969), provides the statistical
test for whether the past values of one variable improve the prediction of another beyond what
its own past values already provide. Although Granger causality does not imply causation in a
philosophical sense, it offers a aimed-at-statistically rigorous characterization of lead-lag
structure that is highly appropriate to the descriptive aims of the current thesis.


Comprehensive technical treatments of VAR estimation, stability checks, and impulse response
identification are provided by Hamilton (Hamilton, 1994) and Lütkepohl (Lütkepohl, 2005),
which are used as the primary references throughout Chapter 4. Supplementary practitioner-
oriented coverage is provided by Enders (Enders, 2015).


2.5.2 Stationarity, Cointegration, and Robust Inference
Stationarity testing is conducted using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test of Dickey and Fuller
(Dickey & Fuller, 1979). When two non-stationary series are integrated of the same order, the
possibility of cointegration must be evaluated before VAR specification, following the
residual-based test of Engle and Granger (Engle & Granger, 1987) and the trace-statistic test
of Johansen (Johansen, 1991).


A particularly important result for the present thesis is established by Sims, Stock and Watson
(Sims et al., 1990), who show that VAR in levels produces consistent estimates for Granger
causality testing even when variables are integrated of order one and not cointegrated. This
finding explains the presence of the VAR-in-levels specification in Chapter 4 after the near
failure in the rejection of cointegration in the Engle-Granger test.


For robust inference in the presence of heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation, Newey and West
(Newey & West, 1987) propose a positive semi-definite covariance estimator that is widely
adopted for time-series regression. The methodological-extension discussion of Chapter 6 cites
this estimator as a robustness test of inference to inference in the regime-dependent residual
environment that is a hallmark of the disruption-freight relationship.




                                                15
2.5.3 Lag Selection
There is a lag length selection in the Vector autoregression specification that is a trade-off
between bias (too few lags) and loss of efficiency (too many lags). The information-criteria
framework discussed by Burnham and Anderson (Burnham & Anderson, 2002) provides the
methodological foundation for this choice. Akaike Information Criterion is desirable where
prediction is required in the model selection whereas Bayesian Information Criterion comes up
with denser models that could be used where the inference framework is adapted. The lag
length employed in this thesis is chosen based on the Akaike Information Criterion as discussed
in section 3, with sensitivity analyses with neighboring lag length values presented in section
4.
2.6 Research Gap and Positioning of the Thesis
The above literature review reveals a research gap. As far as we understand based on the
reviewed literature, no published continuous, monthly news-based index with the
measurements of container shipping disruption intensity specifically which are built with a
large language model and tested against an existing freight benchmark. The nearest precedents
are deficient in this requirement in three different ways.


First, the Economic Policy Uncertainty Index of Baker, Bloom and Davis (Baker et al., 2016a)
provides the methodological template for a news-based index but uses simple keyword
matching and is targeted at macroeconomic policy uncertainty rather than at sector-specific
maritime disruption. The authors themselves recognize the weakness of the use of keywords
(the reason being the presence of the keyword-matching weakness) and this urge to use a more
semantically capable classifier in this thesis.


Second, the LLM-based supply chain risk literature, exemplified by Fang and colleagues (Fang
et al., 2026), focuses on firm-level disclosures rather than on aggregate market-level news
coverage, and does not produce an aggregate index that can be validated against a market
benchmark. The methodological building block which this thesis is based on is the structured-
prompt design that was developed in that literature, although the scope of the empirical data is
different.


Third, the maritime economics literature on COVID-19 disruptions, including Khan and
colleagues (Khan et al., 2022) and the UNCTAD Policy Brief (Unctad, 2021), characterizes
the consequences of specific disruption events but does not offer a continuous measurement

                                                  16
instrument that operates at the monthly frequency and that can be extended naturally to
subsequent disruption episodes.


The present thesis addresses this gap by combining the qualitative taxonomy of Munim
(Munim, 2025) with the structured-prompt LLM methodology of Fang and colleagues (Fang
et al., 2026), validating the resulting monthly index against the Shanghai Containerized Freight
Index in the empirical spirit of Khan and colleagues (Khan et al., 2022), using the vector-
autoregression and Granger-causality framework of Sims (Sims, 1980) and Granger (Granger,
1969). The thesis thereby incorporates qualitative taxonomy literature, the text classification
literature based on LLM, the news-based economic indices literature and the time-series
econometric literature to an all-in-one reproducible measurement system.

3 Methodology
This thesis develops and validates a monthly, news-based disruption index for the container
shipping sector using a three-stage methodological framework. The first stage applies a large
language model (LLM) to classify container-shipping-related news headlines according to their
disruption relevance. The second stage aggregates the headline-level classification outputs into
alternative monthly index formulations and selects the final disruption index. The third stage
evaluates the empirical validity and econometric behaviour of the selected index by comparing
it with the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI), which is used as the external freight-
market benchmark. Figure 1 shows the whole pipeline of construction of the container shipping
disruption index.
The overall research design follows the broader logic of news-based index construction, where
textual information is converted into a continuous quantitative indicator and then assessed
against observable economic or market variables(Baker et al., 2016b; Tetlock, 2007). However,
this study departs from traditional keyword-counting approaches by using contextual LLM-
based classification. This approach is intended to capture the meaning and disruption relevance
of headlines more effectively than simple token-frequency methods, particularly because
container shipping disruptions may be expressed through varied and context-dependent
language(Fang et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2023). Therefore, the methodology combines automated
text interpretation, index construction, and time-series econometric validation within a single
reproducible analytical pipeline. Now, the whole pipeline of construction of the container
shipping disruption index is shown in figure 1.

3.1 Data
The textual dataset used in this study consists of 5,586 container-shipping-related news
headlines with corresponding publication dates covering the period from January 2015 to
December 2025. The publication dates are parsed from a YYYYMMDD integer field.
Observations with missing headline or date values are removed, and the remaining dataset is
arranged chronologically before further processing. This ensures that the subsequent monthly
aggregation and time-series analysis are conducted on a clean and temporally ordered corpus.


The validation benchmark used in the study is the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index
(SCFI), collected at monthly frequency(Khan et al., 2022). The SCFI is selected because it
directly reflects container freight-market conditions and has been used in prior empirical work
on container shipping and pandemic-era freight dynamics. In this thesis, the SCFI is treated as
a defensible market benchmark for validating the constructed disruption index. However, the
choice should be interpreted as a methodological decision rather than as a claim that SCFI is
the only or universally accepted benchmark for container freight-market disruption.


3.2 Stage 1: LLM-Based Headline Classification
3.2.1 Classification Framework
In the first stage, each news headline is classified using an LLM-based scoring framework.
Every headline is assigned a disruption relevance score on a 0–10 ordinal scale, where higher
values indicate stronger disruption relevance. In addition, each headline is assigned to one or
more disruption themes(Munim, 2025) and tagged with an aggregate dimension: shipping,
economic, both, or none.


The classification framework is based on the cross-domain disruption taxonomy developed by
Munim (2025). This taxonomy consists of two aggregate dimensions, seven second-order
themes, and seventeen first-order codes(Braun & Clarke, 2006). The complete taxonomy is
inserted into the model prompt so that the LLM performs classification within a clearly defined
domain-specific structure(Loughran & McDonald, 2011). This prompt-based domain
adaptation is important because general-purpose textual approaches may misclassify
specialized shipping and financial language. Instead of relying on a fixed keyword dictionary,
this study uses structured prompting to guide the model toward context-sensitive interpretation
of container-shipping disruption signals.


                                              27
3.2.2 Model and Inference Configuration
Headline classification is performed using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, an open-source instruction-
tuned LLM derived from the Qwen model family(Yang & Qwen, 2024). The use of an open-
source model supports greater reproducibility because the model version can be fixed and
reused, unlike proprietary API-based systems where model behaviour may change over
time(Lopez-Lira & Tang, 2023).


The model is loaded in float16 precision with automatic device mapping and executed in a
Kaggle dual-T4 GPU environment. Inference is conducted in batches of five headlines per
model call. The generation settings are fixed as follows: max_new_tokens = 2048, temperature
= 0.1, top_p = 0.9, and do_sample = True. The low temperature setting is used to reduce output
variability and produce near-deterministic responses. However, because sampling remains
enabled, the output cannot be considered fully deterministic at the bit level.


3.2.3 Prompt Structure and Output Parsing
The model prompt contains four fixed components that remain unchanged across all 1,118
classification batches. These components are: the full disruption taxonomy, a 0–10 scoring
rubric with anchored descriptions, a constrained vocabulary of valid theme labels, and a strict
JSON output schema. This structured prompting strategy is used to increase consistency,
improve interpretability, and make the classification output suitable for downstream
quantitative analysis(Fang et al., 2026).


The model responses are parsed using a two-step fallback procedure. First, the parser attempts
to identify and extract the complete JSON array from the model output. If this fails, a secondary
strategy extracts individual JSON objects using regular expressions. When fewer than five
objects are successfully recovered from a batch, the missing entries are replaced with zero-
score placeholders to preserve alignment between the original headline list and the
classification output. Progress is checkpointed every twenty batches to reduce the risk of data
loss under the computational session limit.


3.2.4 Classification Output
The LLM classification produced a highly sparse disruption-score distribution. Out of 5,586
headlines, 4,222 headlines, or 75.6%, received a score of 0. A further 569 headlines, or 10.2%,
received scores between 1 and 3; 269 headlines, or 4.8%, received scores between 4 and 6; and

                                               28
526 headlines, or 9.4%, received scores between 7 and 10. This distribution indicates that a
large share of the original news corpus was not directly disruption-relevant, which justifies the
use of noise-resistant monthly aggregation procedures in the second stage.


The final classification output also contains 849 zero-score placeholders, of which 847 are
marked as missing and 2 are marked as parse errors. These placeholders represent
approximately 15.2% of all headlines and 20.1% of all zero-score observations. Therefore,
placeholder zeros are not treated as equivalent to genuine zero-disruption classifications. They
are acknowledged as a potential source of measurement error and should be considered when
interpreting the reliability of the final index.


3.3 Stage 2: Monthly Index Construction
3.3.1 Candidate Index Formulations
After headline-level classification, the disruption scores are aggregated to monthly frequency.
Eight base index formulations are constructed to capture different theoretical dimensions of
disruption intensity and disruption volume. These formulations include a raw mean-intensity
measure(Tetlock, 2007), a raw volume-sum measure(Baker et al., 2016b), relevant-only
intensity measures, intensity-by-volume formulations, logarithmic and square-root volume-
scaled measures(Manning et al., 2008), a high-impact headline ratio, and a shipping-theme
coverage count based on the disruption taxonomy.


A three-month exponential moving average is then applied to each base formulation to reduce
short-term editorial noise and month-to-month volatility(Arratia et al., 2021). This procedure
generates sixteen candidate index series in total: eight unsmoothed base series and eight
corresponding smoothed series. The purpose of constructing multiple candidate formulations
is to evaluate which aggregation method best reflects observable freight-market disruption
while maintaining a theoretically defensible relationship between headline intensity, headline
frequency, and market conditions.


3.3.2 Selection of the Final Index
The final disruption index is selected as the candidate formulation with the highest absolute
Pearson correlation with log-SCFI. The selected formulation is:

In this formulation, 𝑠‾𝑡 denotes the mean disruption score of all headlines in month 𝑡, 𝑛𝑡 denotes
the monthly headline count, and EMA3 denotes the three-month exponential moving average

This formulation combines three components. The mean score term captures the average
disruption intensity of the monthly news flow. The square-root volume term introduces
sublinear scaling, allowing the index to account for headline volume while preventing high-
volume months with weak disruption signals from dominating the index. The three-month
EMA smooths temporary fluctuations and reduces the influence of short-term editorial noise.


3.4 Stage 3: Econometric Validation
3.4.1 Stationarity and Cointegration
The econometric validation begins by examining the time-series properties of the disruption
index and the freight benchmark. Augmented Dickey–Fuller tests(Dickey & Fuller, 1979)
indicate that both the disruption index and log-SCFI are integrated of order one, I(1), in levels
and become stationary after first differencing. The raw, non-logged SCFI series tests as
stationary in levels, which motivates the use of log-SCFI in the bivariate econometric analysis
so that both variables share the same integration order.


3.4.2 VAR Specification
Because the disruption index and log-SCFI are I(1) but not cointegrated, the study estimates a
bivariate Vector Autoregression model in levels(Sims et al., 1990). The VAR framework treats
both variables as potentially endogenous and allows the dynamic interaction between the
disruption index and the freight benchmark to be examined without imposing a single
dependent-independent variable structure(Sims, 1980).


Lag length is selected using the Akaike Information Criterion(Burnham & Anderson, 2002),
which selects a six-lag specification. The fitted VAR model satisfies the eigenvalue stability
condition, indicating that the estimated system is dynamically stable. This specification is then
used for Granger-causality testing, impulse-response analysis, and forecast error variance
decomposition.




                                               30
3.4.3 Granger-Causality Analysis
Granger-causality tests(Granger, 1969) are conducted in both directions across lags 1–6. The
tests are performed using both level variables and first-differenced variables to assess whether
the direction of predictive association is robust to specification choice.

In the levels specification, log-SCFI Granger-causes the disruption index at lags 1–4, with p-
values ranging from 0.0044 to 0.0443. In contrast, the disruption index does not Granger-cause
log-SCFI at lags 1–5, although the smallest p-value appears at lag 6 with (p = 0.0857).


3.4.4 Impulse Responses and Forecast Error Variance Decomposition
Impulse response functions are estimated over a twelve-month horizon to examine the dynamic
response of each variable to shocks in the VAR system(Lütkepohl, 2005). A forecast error
variance decomposition is also conducted to quantify the proportion of each variable’s forecast
error variance explained by shocks to itself and to the other variable.


The orthogonalized variance decomposition uses Cholesky identification with the disruption
index ordered first. Under this ordering, freight shocks explain an increasing share of the
disruption index’s forecast error variance, rising from approximately 1% at horizon 1 to about
20% at horizon 11. Similarly, disruption-index shocks explain an increasing share of freight
variance, rising from approximately 4% at horizon 1 to about 36% at horizon 11.


3.4.5 Diagnostic Tests and Regime Analysis
Diagnostic tests are conducted to evaluate the adequacy of the estimated VAR model. The
residuals satisfy the Durbin–Watson criterion, and the Ljung–Box test at lag 10 does not
indicate residual autocorrelation. These results suggest that the six-lag VAR specification
sufficiently captures the serial dependence structure of the data.


However, the Jarque–Bera test rejects normality for the disruption-index equation residuals,
which show skewness of 1.67 and excess kurtosis of 4.09. In contrast, normality is not rejected
for the freight-equation residuals. This non-normality implies that analytical impulse-response
confidence bands should be interpreted cautiously, and bootstrap-based confidence intervals
would be preferable for robustness. Where heteroscedasticity or residual autocorrelation
remains a concern, heteroscedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent covariance estimation
provides an appropriate robustness extension.


                                                31
Finally, a rolling 24-month Pearson correlation is computed between the disruption index and
the SCFI to examine regime dependence. The rolling correlation is strongly positive during the
2021–2022 and 2023–2024 crisis windows, while it is near zero or negative during calmer
periods.


4 Results and Discussion
The chapter introduces and describes the empirical findings of the construction and validation
of the container shipping disruption index based on the LLM. The analysis will not merely
demonstrate whether the recommended index follows the freight market conditions or not, but
it will also analyze how the relationship will vary with time and whether the index further
follows freight price trends or leads.


The results are organized into six parts. To start with, the results of the headline level of the
LLM classification are addressed. Second, the chosen monthly disruption index is compared
to the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI). Third, it reports its stationarity and
cointegration findings as justifications to the time-series specification. Fourth, the results of the
Granger-causality are analyzed to determine the predictive direction of disruption coverage on
freight rates. The fifth one is that dynamic interactions between the two variables are explored
with the help of impulse response functions and forecast error variance decomposition. Lastly,
the results of rolling-correlation and diagnostic are explained to gauge regime dependence and
model reliability.


4.1 Headline-Level Classification Results
The initial step of the research involved categorizing 5,586 news headings related to container-
shipping with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. The disruption relevance scores of each headline were
determined as 0 to 10: the lowest rating of 0 corresponds to absence of meaningful disruption
relevance, and 10 is associated with a perfect overlap with events of disruption of container
shipping. The model also attributed each headline to either a single or several disruption themes
according to a taxonomy chosen in the methodology chapter.


The output of the classification indicates that the majority of headlines were not closely
connected with the disruption in container shipping. Out of 5,586 headlines, 4,222 headlines,
or 75.6%, received a score of 0. Another 569 headlines or 10.2 were scored between 1 and 3,
                                                 32
meaning they were tangentially relevant. Headlines that had moderate relevance scores until
269 or 4.8 were defined by a moderation score. There were only 526 high relevance headlines
with scores 7-10 9.4% high relevance. Figure 2 shows the score distribution of LLM-Based
headline classification.




               Figure 2: Score Distribution of LLM-Based Headline Classification

This distribution can be used to interpret the index. This large proportion of zero-score
headlines implies that the original news corpus has a lot of noise. That is to say, numerous
headlines do not directly state how container shipping has been disrupted, by stating that there
is trade, shipping, markets, or that the economy is experiencing good or poor times. A plain
count-based index would thus threaten the count of all items of news as being equally
informative. An LLM-powered scoring process can decrease this issue by differentiating the
most relevant headlines of disruption by outstanding or incidental news reports.


In the same breath, a weakness is also present in the classification output. Because a majority
of the headlines are rated at zero, any parsing error or padding of a zero introduced at the step
of extracting the JSON can be hard to separate with a valid zero-score headline. Thus, the index
can be seen as encoded machine-based measurement of disruption severity, rather than a
completely human-validated set of disruption data. Benchmark Confidence in the accuracy of
the classification would be enhanced with a benchmark developed by human coders in the
future.

4.3 Association between the Disruption Index and the Freight Rates.
The first validation result shows a strong positive association between the proposed disruption
index and the SCFI. The last index reached the Pearson correlation of 0.7928 with the SCFI in
levels and 0.8431 with the log-SCFI. The rank Spearman correlation stood at 0.8665 and this
suggests that in addition to the relationship being linear, it is also very strong monotonic.


This finding implies that the index powered by LLM is able to measure a signal of disruption
that is best associated with freight market conditions. During shipping stress periods with a
sharp increase in freight rates, the news coverage of disruption also tends to escalate. On the
contrary, disruption coverage decreases when freight market pressure is less.

cross-correlation between the disruption index and the freight index. The correlation is not only
positive but is also statistically significant across lags-12, +12 months. The highest level

= 0.7928) which is near to each other. The result of the difference between lag 0 and lag +1 is
extremely low, thus the result may be interpreted as an almost contemporaneous relationship

4.4 Stationarity and Cointegration Results
Prior to estimating the VAR model, there were tests on stationarity and cointegration. The
Augmented Dickey-Fuller tests show that both the disruption index and log-SCFI can be
identified as non-stationary at the levels, but they are stationary after the first difference. This
implies that both variables may be considered to be integrated of order one, or (I(1)).

                                                35
Further, the model was tested on cointegration with the help of the Engle-Granger residual-
based test and the Johansen trace test. The Engle-Granger test did not reject H0 : no
cointegration, and even the Johansen test failed to establish a cointegrating relationship
according to the specifications of the tests. Thus, the findings are inconclusive when it comes
to the relation of stable long-run equilibrium between the disruption index and log-SCFI.


There are two implications of this finding. First, the relationship of the disruption index and
freight rates evolves concurrently during significant occurrences, but these variables are not
interrelated significantly to be considered as a long-run equilibrium. Second, since the variables
are (I(1)) and not cointegrated, a VAR in levels was fitted to make inferences about Granger-
causality, according to the justification in terms of time-series, as justification of the
methodology chapter. Akaike Information Criterion was used to pick the lag order and resulted
in the selection of six lags. The stability condition is an estimated condition of the VAR(6)
system, that is, the dynamic system can be analyzed with impulse response and variance
decomposition.

4.9 VAR Diagnostic Results
The diagnostic tests reveal that the VAR model is quite acceptable, but not ideal, in general.
The equations have values near to 2 as the value of Durbin-Watson is 1.9975 in disruption
equation and 2.0855 in log-freight equation. This indicates that there is no severe first-order
autocorrelation issue.
This is also given by the Ljung Box test lag 10. The p-values of the disruption equation and
log-freight equation are 0.8511 and 0.7704, respectively, which implies that they have no
evidence of residual serial correlation. The stability testing also reveals that the VAR system
is steady with the maximum eigenvalue modulus of 0.8882, less than 1.
Nonetheless, the findings of the residual normality are conflicting. Jarque-Bra test is non-
significant to reject the normality test, and the p-value is 0.0000. The disruption residuals are
both positively skewed and leptokurtic, with skew of 1.644 and excess kurtosis of 3.857. This
is not because of coincidence, since disruption data are likely to display crisis-induced spikes,
and not normal variation. The log-freight equation is much better and Jarque-Ber p-value is
0.0544 which is a little above 5%.
The diagnostic findings thus point to the idea that the VAR reflects the fundamental dynamic
structure with no severe residual autocorrelation, although care should be taken with the
inference on the basis of normality. Robustness checks that could help in the future would be
bootstrap confidence intervals and the use of heteroscedasticity-robust inference.


4.10 Overall Discussion
The findings demonstrate that the proposed LLM-based disruption index can serve well as a
market-aligned disruption measure of container shipping. This is well correlated to SCFI and
log-SCFI, which shows that the index is a measure of a signal which is very close to freight
market stress. The score distribution also helps understand why the LLM-based classification
comes in handy: the majority of headlines presented in the corpus are not disruptive directly,
hence simply count a keyword or count by volume is likely to be a noisy method.


The econometric findings enhance this remark. The index should not be thought of as a major
indicator of freight rates. In the levels based Granger-causality tests, the freight rate is the first
to trigger the disruption index implying media disruption coverage is the product of realised
freight market stress. This agrees with the fact that the news coverage regularly illustrates the
state of the market as it is revealed but never leads to the creation of the conditions.


                                                 42
Simultaneously, the rolling correlation findings demonstrate that the index is very practical in
the disruption regimes. In low volatility times, the connection between news disruption
coverage and freight rates is weak. In periods of crisis, there is a very strong relationship. This
implies that the index is particularly handy as a check-up tool in times of stress in the supply-
chain.


The disruption index predicting freight price thus is not the major contribution of this chapter.
Instead, the input is that a focused, taxonomy-infused LLM pipeline can process a public news
headline to become a continual monthly value that predictively predicts real-world episodes of
container shipping disruption in the real world. This offers an interpretable and reproducible
instrument to maritime economists, logistics managers, freight forwarders, and policymakers.
The time-series tests established that both the disruption index and the log-SCFI were non-
stationary at the level and stationary at the first differences but there are no signs of
cointegration. There was estimation of a stable VAR (6) model. The results of Granger-
causality indicated that log-SCFI Granger-causes the disruption index in level Granger-
causalities at lags 1 to 4, whereas the other way is not significant in levels. The results of
impulse response and FEVD justified a robust dynamic relationship, but structural
interpretation is not feasible due to the limiting sensitivity of ordering.


5 Conclusions and Recommendations
5.1 Conclusions
In this thesis, the disruption index for the container shipping was constructed based on monthly
news information and validated with the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI). The
goal was to create a measurement tool that could be replicated and establish a meaningful way
to quantify disruption to the container shipping industry from public news headlines.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is used to classify 5,586 news headlines related to container shipping
from January 2015 to December 2025. Each headline was scored by level of disruption
relevance based on the disruption taxonomy developed by Munim (2025) and plotted on the
disruption themes. This is more than just a number count of keywords – the model was able to
grasp the meaning of a headline in the context of the maritime supply-chain.
The monthly index obtained from sixteen formulas was found to be:


                                     𝐷𝐼𝑡 = 𝐸𝑀𝐴3 [𝑠̅𝑡 × √𝑛𝑡 ]


                                                43
with monthly mean disruption score 𝑠̅𝑡 , and monthly mean headline count 𝑛𝑡 . The square-root
volume term(√𝑛𝑡 ) minimized the impact of any month with high volume and low signal news,
and the three-month exponential moving average played a smoothing role to dampen the nearly
daily editorial noise of Twitter and news.
The empirical results show that the constructed disruption index has a strong association with
freight-market conditions. Overall, the Pearson correlation was 0.7928 with the SCFI in levels,
0.8431 for log-SCFI and the Spearman rank correlation was 0.8665. These findings suggest
that the index is effective for capturing the large-scale trends in the intensity of disturbance in
container shipping.


The econometric validation also revealed that the indices (disruption index and log-SCFI) are
both of order one, and that the results of the cointegration tests do not indicate a stable long-
run relationship between the two. For this reason, a VAR in levels was applied to be able to
carry out a dynamic analysis. According to the Granger-causality results, log-SCFI
significantly Granger-causes the disruption index at lags 1-4 while the disruption index does
not Granger-cause log-SCFI at the 5% significance level. This suggests that freight-rate
developments in general, rather than the index itself, serve as a good indicator to the
developments of media disruption coverage.
The impulse response and variance decomposition findings are in line with this interpretation.
Thus, the reaction of the disruption index to freight shocks is more like a standard one-step-
ahead response of freight rates to disruption-index shocks. However, care needs to be taken in
the interpretation of the results from FEVD as the orthogonalization decomposition is
dependent on Cholesky ordering. The 24-month window correlation test also reveals that the
correlation with freight rates is highest during major disruptions in particular: the 2021-2022
shipping container crisis as well as the 2023-2024 Red Sea disruption period, while lower
during more tranquil times.


In summary this thesis can be regarded as a methodological advancement through the
development of an LLM approach for measuring maritime disruption, as an empirical
validation of the index to SCFI, and can be regarded as a practical suggestion for a low-cost
tool to monitor the intensity of maritime disruption. The index should be used, however, as a




                                                44
tool to measure and be aware of the freight-rate regimes rather than to be treated as a single
freight rate forecasting model.


5.2 Recommendations
There are a number of recommendations that will enhance the reliability and utility of this work
for future research.
First, the results should be used to validate the classification of the LLM-created texts. Among
all of the above, it is recommended that the LLM scores be compared to human judgments by
manually labelling a sample of headlines with domain experts and by calculating agreement
measures like accuracy, correlation or interrater reliability. This will directly evaluate the
accuracy of disruption relevance classification of the model. Second, in future research, cross
model robustness tests are needed. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct has been the sole size and model used
in this thesis. Testing should also be done with other open source models to investigate whether
the index is stable between different model families. Repeated runs keeping the settings for the
decoding the same should also be used to test the consistency of the scoring. Third, index
selection needs to be enriched using an out-of-sample validation process. Future studies should
include a hold out period or other benchmarks to minimize their selection-on-outcome bias in
the analyses of selecting the final index, as the index was partially selected according to its
relationship with SCFI. This would increase the rigor in the validation. Fourth, future studies
ought to incorporate other maritime signals like port congestion data, container availability
data, schedule reliability, Drewry World Container Index, Freightos Baltic Index or regional
freight indices. This would gauge how well the index reflects a shipping disruption in general,
or just SCFI-related movements. Fifth, there should be greater econometric robustness. Future
research will involve experiments with other VAR lag sizes, VAR in differences, impulse
response confidence intervals using bootstraps, different ordering of the Cholesky and
heteroscedasticity-autocorrelation-consistent standard errors. However such checks are
particularly important since the residuals of the disruption-equation were found not to be
normally distributed. Last, the index can be expanded from the headlines by using the full text
of news articles, multilingual shipping news, port information and official maritime news. This
would give more textual detail and could potentially enhance the detection of disruption
narratives sooner, and more accurately, for the index. Finally, this study proposes a disruption
index to help systematically measure disruption in container shipping based on the news and
suggests it is a useful first step. The greatest benefit of it is in following-up on the intensity of
disturbance, provision of information on crisis regimes and information for decision making in
                                                 45
shipping. Future research includes human validation and robustness of the models as well as
more extensive benchmarks and out-of-sample testing.
